# Measuring what open-model inference actually costs

### What changes for an enterprise when a lab ships open weights — and what does not

**Hariprasad Sudharshan** · August 2026 · Working paper **v3** · [opentoken](https://tokencalci.onrender.com)

---

## Abstract

I built a calculator to price self-hosting an open-weight model against renting the same weights from a neocloud API. It told me that running a dense model yourself costs **4 to 6 times** what a provider charges. I published that figure. Then I measured it properly and it was wrong — my fleet-sizing had assumed adding GPUs adds throughput, which it does not.

v1 was that correction. External review found the correction had made the same class of error : I replaced one wrong number with a **floor** and then quoted the floor as a **cost**. v2 separated them. This version adds what reviewers said was actually missing — **where every number comes from, which numbers are assumptions, and what any of it means on the day a lab ships weights.**

Priced at the precision providers actually serve, across five representative models :

- **bare compute runs 0.15× to 1.80×** the neocloud rate — for four of five, the silicon is *cheaper* than renting
- **fully loaded cost runs 1.7× to 8.2×** — the same fleet, with idle time, people and overhead put back

**The gap is not the silicon. It is everything wrapped around the silicon.**

Three further results, each revised downward under review :

1. **Per-model prices are not falling fast.** A fixed basket of 19 models moved between **0.976×/yr and 0.840×/yr** over 18 months depending on the index formula. Nothing resembling "10×/year" survives any estimator.
2. **Tensor parallelism buys capacity, not speed.** A **modelling assumption that fits the data, not a measurement** — v1 called it measured, which it is not.
3. **Price dispersion beats every modelling choice here.** The same weights list from **$0.14 to $3.00** per million tokens. Which provider you sign with moves the answer more than fleet sizing, precision and duty cycle combined.

Every figure is regenerated by one committed script against pinned snapshots. **Appendix A** lists every input with its source, URL, refresh cadence and confidence grade. **Appendix B** lists every derived quantity with the assumptions behind it. I would rather be corrected than cited.

**Disclosure :** No funding, no sponsorship, no commercial relationship with any provider named here. The paper argues for renting inference, which is a vendor-shaped conclusion, so it matters that every number I have revised has moved **against** my own prior position — three times now.

## 1. Why bother measuring this

Every time a lab publishes weights, the discussion moves from frontier to open as though the hard part had shipped. What shipped is the weights.

An open weight is a file. A served token is a system. Between them sit roughly ten blocks — precision, KV cache, batching, kernels, parallelism, fleet, reliability, economics, API surface — and exactly one of them arrives in the download.

We have been here before. In 2008 we had the source code and thought we had the product. It took most of a decade to name the missing work DevOps, and only after naming it did anyone budget for it. Model serving is at that stage now.

## 2. What actually changes when a lab ships weights

This section is new in v3 because it is the question people were actually asking, and the rest of the paper was answering a narrower one.

### 2.1 Three stages that get called the same thing

A release triggers three different activities. They are routinely discussed as one, they measure **different quantities**, and the numbers from one do not transfer to the next.

| | **Demo** | **Experimentation** | **Deployment** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question | Does it run? | Is it good enough for *our* task? | Can we serve it, at our load, within our constraints? |
| Timescale | Hours | Weeks | Quarters |
| Concurrency | 1 | 1–8 | Hundreds |
| What is measured | Latency for one | Task quality | **Throughput for many**, p95, availability |
| Typical hardware | Laptop, or one rented GPU | A few rented GPUs | A fleet sized for peak |
| Typical cost | ~$0 | Tens to hundreds of dollars | Six to seven figures a year |
| Blocks of the chain touched | 1 of 10 | 2–3 of 10 | 10 of 10 |
| Decision it can support | "This is worth an experiment" | **Model selection** | **Architecture and budget** |
| Decision it cannot support | Anything about cost | Anything about scale | — |

**The trap is between column one and column three, and it is arithmetic.** A laptop demo measures how fast a model answers *one* person. A production bill is set by how many tokens a fleet delivers to *many* people at once. On a 7B model at fp16 those differ by roughly **100×** — about 39 tok/s for a single stream on high-bandwidth laptop memory, against roughly 3,900 tok/s aggregate on one H100 at batch 256 [2].

Both numbers are real. They are not the same measurement, and the demo is the one that feels finished. That is why a working demo so often produces a budget that is wrong by two orders of magnitude — the demo optimised the one property that does not scale.

*Derived, see Appendix B-7. The laptop figure is a bandwidth-bound estimate, not a benchmark I ran.*

### 2.2 What moves the needle on release day

Ranked by how much each changes an enterprise's cost or feasibility, from this tool's own numbers.

**1. Does it fit on one card at the precision it ships in?** This is the single largest lever and it is usually absent from launch coverage. A model needing one GPU per replica and a model needing eleven differ by an order of magnitude in fleet cost for identical tokens. In the current catalogue, **27 of 55 models (49%) fit on one H100** at their served precision.

**2. Did the lab ship quantisation-aware weights?** Precision changes the fleet size for **18 of 55 models** in this catalogue versus evaluating everything at fp16. A QAT or FP8/MXFP4 checkpoint released *by the lab* is worth more to an enterprise than a few points of benchmark score, because it moves the model across the VRAM boundary with calibration the lab has already validated. gpt-oss-120b ships MXFP4 and fits one 80GB card; scored at fp16 the same model demands four.

**3. How fast do providers pick it up.** For most enterprises the realistic price of an open model is **what a neocloud charges to serve it**, not what it costs to self-host. Six of 55 catalogue models have no live provider match at all — for those, "open" means you self-host by default and pay the full premium. Provider availability within days of release is therefore a first-order commercial fact, and it is not something the lab controls.

**4. Licence terms for commercial use and derivative works.** Binary, and it decides whether the rest of the analysis happens.

**5. KV-cache architecture.** GQA, MLA, sliding-window and hybrid designs determine how much concurrency a given card supports, which sets replica count. *This tool cannot currently price it — see Appendix B-3, and treat that as a known gap rather than an argument that it does not matter.*

**What moves the needle less than the coverage implies :** headline parameter count (VRAM at the *served* precision is the operative number), a few points of benchmark delta (below the resolution of most procurement decisions), and "runs on a laptop" (see 2.1).

### 2.3 The honest read for an enterprise

Open weights change **who you can buy from** far more than they change **what it costs**. The download removes vendor lock-in and licence risk. It does not remove the serving cost, because the serving cost was never mostly the weights — sections 5 and 6 show it is mostly idle capacity and people.

So the release-day question is not "should we self-host this now". It is: *does this model fit on one card, who is serving it by Friday, and what does that do to our negotiating position.*

## 3. Method and provenance

Fourteen data layers, each graded by how directly a claim traces to a record. **Appendix A** is the full table with URLs and limitations; this is the summary.

| Grade | Meaning | Layers |
|---|---|---|
| **measured** | Fetched from a dated feed; we can point at the exact record | 4 |
| **derived** | Computed by us from a dated feed; the method is published | 3 |
| **published** | A real figure from a named third party, not machine-fetched | 6 |
| **estimate** | Our own judgement. The weakest class, flagged wherever it appears | 1 |

The rule the tool enforces on itself : *layers marked `estimate` are our own judgement and are flagged wherever they appear. Do not present them as measurements.* v1 and v2 both broke this rule — v1 graded the parallelism claim `measured` when it is an assumption, and v2 still carried three inflated grades in the cost-decomposition panel. Both are corrected here and in the tool.

**The economic model** rests on one asymmetry. Self-hosting is a **fixed** cost: you provision for peak and pay 24×7 whether tokens flow or not. An API is **variable**: you pay per token and idle is free. So the deciding variable is the duty cycle, and a workload stated as requests-per-day converts to peak-and-duty exactly, since `duty = 1 / peak-to-average ratio`. That identity is exact, not a fudge — Appendix B-1.

## 4. Finding one : per-model prices are flat, but how flat depends on the formula

Two different things get called "the price falling" and they differ by roughly 20×.

**Fixed basket** — the same 19 models present at both ends of the window, 2025-01-13 to 2026-07-14 :

| Index | 18-month total | Annualised | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carli — arithmetic mean of price relatives | 0.964× | **0.976×/yr** | *v1 reported only this* |
| Jevons — geometric mean | 0.770× | **0.840×/yr** | the standard choice [9] |
| Median relative | 1.000× | **1.000×/yr** | |

*Source: git history of the LiteLLM price file [5], recovered and normalised through the same code path as live prices. `derived`, Appendix A-4.*

**Whats the use of showing three :**

v1 said "essentially flat" and quoted 0.976. That is the **Carli index**, which has documented upward bias and fails the time-reversal test; statistical agencies moved off it for exactly this reason [9]. My basket is the shape that maximises that bias — two models fell 8–11× and are floored at zero in an arithmetic mean, two doubled and are unbounded above.

Under Jevons the same data says −16%/year, which is not "essentially flat". I did not pick Carli to flatter the result; I picked it without knowing there was a choice, which is worse and more common.

**What survives all three :** nothing resembling 10×/year. That the field's claim is wrong is the finding. Its magnitude is not.

**Cheapest available.** The lowest-priced model in the catalogue fell from **$0.100 to $0.040** per million input tokens, about **46%/year** — but this is the minimum of a *growing* set, which is monotonically non-increasing by construction, and the whole move is one step in September 2025. A direction, not a rate.

Both series are true and answer different questions. Prices fall because *cheaper new models keep arriving*, not because the model you picked gets cheaper. Commit to one model and the API side will not drift away from you the way the folklore implies. Re-platform quarterly and you capture the fast rate — and re-platforming is engineering work nobody costs.

**A caveat that may be fatal to the magnitude.** The basket is fixed in model identity, not in sampling frame. Provider observations inside it grew **46 → 141**. Split it: models that gained ≥3× providers have a geomean relative of **0.404**; models with stable provider counts, **1.038**. The series partly sorts on *how many cheap listings entered each median window*, not on price. I do not think this overturns the direction; I do think the number measures something other than what I claimed, and a corrected version needs provider fixed effects I have not built.

## 5. Finding two : tensor parallelism buys capacity, not speed

This invalidated my own headline number, and it is also the weakest-evidenced claim in the paper. Both are true.

| Model | Active | GPUs | Total tok/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qwen3 30B-A3B | 3B | 1 | 3,900 |
| Gemma 4 31B | 31B | 1 | 3,100 |
| Mistral Small 24B | 24B | 2 | 3,400 |
| Llama 3.3 70B | 70B | 8 | 2,800 |

*Source: published vLLM serving benchmarks, 256 concurrent, 512 in / 512 out [2]. `published` — benchmarks someone else ran. Appendix A-9.*

Eight GPUs do not deliver eight times the tokens. They deliver roughly what one does, because tensor-parallel decode pays collective communication on every layer and that cost cancels the bandwidth added. **You split a model across GPUs to make it fit, not to make it fast.** To serve more tokens you deploy another full replica and pay again.

My original model grew a single fleet until it was fast enough — silently assuming width bought speed — and demanded roughly 2.4× the hardware a workload needs. That is where the missing 4–6× lived.

### 5.1 What is wrong with the evidence

v1 presented this as measured. **It is not.**

**The anchors are confounded by construction.** Model size and GPU count move together — the small models are the one-GPU rows, the big model is the eight-GPU row. No anchor varies GPU count while holding model and precision fixed, which is the only experiment that tests the claim.

**A constant fits nearly as well.** Predicting a flat 3,275 tok/s for every row scores 1.11 geomean error. The two-parameter fit scores 1.02. Two free parameters buy eight points on four observations spanning 2,800–3,900.

**The only same-model GPU-count comparison contradicts the claim, and it is the row I excluded.** Llama 3.1 70B at fp8 on **one** H100 : 460 tok/s observed [3], 5,603 predicted — a **12.2× miss**. I set it aside as a different harness and batch size. That confound is real and it is not 12× wide.

### 5.2 The fit, reported honestly

v1 claimed "9 of 10 benchmarks at geometric-mean error 1.054×". Arithmetically true, rhetorically false — five of the nine are the points the parameters were fitted on and score 1.000 by construction.

| Points | n | Geomean error |
|---|---|---|
| Fit points (algebraic identities, not predictions) | 4 | 1.000× |
| **Genuinely held out**, excluding the declared large-MoE blind spot | **5** | **1.099×** |
| Held out, including it | 6 | 1.414× |
| Excluded : large MoE (DeepSeek V3) | 1 | 4.99× |
| Excluded : cross-harness, batch 64, 1 GPU | 1 | **12.18×** |

Worst genuinely held-out residual is A100 at **1.38×** — Ampere has no FP8 tensor cores, so that error is architecture, not bandwidth, and the model has no term for it. The bandwidth exponent is itself fitted on **one pair** (B200/H100); pairwise exponents across the four cards span 0.699–1.396.

**What would settle it :** one model, one precision, TP degrees 1/2/4/8, identical hardware and batch, interconnect topology recorded. About $5 of rented H100 time. Until then this is a modelling assumption, and the tool now says so.

### 5.3 The Tensor Parallel Tax, and its domain

> **TPT — Tensor Parallel Tax**
> `TPT = ceil( (total params × bytes per param × 1.3) / GPU VRAM )`
> The GPUs one replica needs, purely to hold the model.

**Where the 1.3 comes from :** a flat 30% allowance for KV cache and activations. It is **an assumption I chose**, not a measurement — Appendix B-3. It reads no context length, batch size, concurrency or attention architecture.

At the benchmark configuration these numbers come from — 1K sequence, 256 concurrent, 70B with GQA — required headroom is about **1.61×**, not 1.30. So **TPT is wrong at its own anchor configuration**, and at production context lengths it is wrong by an order of magnitude.

Worse : because the allowance is a fraction of *weight bytes*, quantising a model **shrinks** its KV budget. gpt-oss-120b is credited with 71 GB of headroom at fp16 and 19 GB at int4 — the inverse of why anyone quantises. Every "self-host wins once you quantise" verdict is therefore computed on a fleet that could not hold the concurrency it is credited with.

**TPT as published is a weights-fit formula.** Useful as that; not a capacity model; the name over-claims. A defensible version needs `f(ctx, concurrency, kv_heads, head_dim, precision)` [1], and the inputs are already in the catalogue.

## 6. Finding three : the cost multiple, floor and loaded

At 200,000 requests/day of 2,000 in / 500 out, business-hours traffic (33% duty), H100s, 36-month amortisation, priced at the precision providers actually serve :

| Model | Served at | TPT | Fleet | Bare compute | Loaded |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| gpt-oss-120b | int4 | 1× | 2 | **0.15×** | 1.7× |
| Qwen3 30B-A3B | fp8 | 1× | 3 | **0.26×** | 2.6× |
| Gemma 4 31B | fp16 | 2× | 12 | **0.49×** | 2.5× |
| Llama 3.3 70B | fp8 | 2× | 8 | **0.54×** | 3.7× |
| DeepSeek V3.2 | fp8 | 11× | 33 | **1.80×** | 8.2× |

*Regenerate with `npm run paper:figures`. Prices [5] as of 2026-08-24; GPU rental [7]; serving precision [6]. Full configuration printed by the script.*

**Bare compute** is the GPU line alone, at 100% utilisation, nobody paid, no overhead, no idle. **Loaded** is what you actually pay. v1 quoted the first and called it the second.

**Two things follow, and the second is the paper's real result.**

*First, precision decides the fleet.* The same table at fp16 — v1's basis throughout — reads 4× TPT and 8.7× loaded for gpt-oss-120b, and 22× TPT and 30.9× loaded for DeepSeek. Scoring a model at a precision it was never released in punishes exactly the quantisation-aware work that gets it under a VRAM boundary.

*Second : the silicon is not the problem.* For four of five models bare compute is **cheaper** than renting. The premium is created almost entirely by what surrounds it — idle capacity, staffing, overhead, redundancy. A provider is not beating you on kernels. They are beating you on **pooling uncorrelated demand across thousands of customers**, which is a property of their customer base, not their engineering.

That is a better statement of "open weights are not open inference" than the one I published. The missing decade is not silicon and not mostly software. It is the operational and demand-shaped layer a download cannot contain.

## 7. Finding four : dispersion beats every modelling choice here

v1 claimed "two independent price feeds disagree on 22% of models, worst 5.7×". Recomputed, with the checks I should have run first :

| | |
|---|---|
| Comparable models | 37 |
| Disagree by more than 1.5× | **7 (19%)**, worst Mistral NeMo at 5.00× |
| **Thin on one side (n ≤ 2)** | **21 of 37** |
| **LiteLLM medians containing an OpenRouter row** | **10 of 37** |

Both caveats cut against the claim and neither was in v1. **The feeds are not independent** — OpenRouter is itself a provider inside the LiteLLM median for 10 of these models, so "independent cross-check" must be withdrawn. And **most of the disagreement is sample size**: with 21 of 37 thin on one side, comparing one observation against a median of eighteen says nothing about the market.

**What survives is better sampled and more useful.** Dispersion *within* a single model is enormous : DeepSeek V3.2 spans **$0.14 to $3.00** per million input tokens across 39 listings from 13 providers; Llama 3.3 70B, $0.12–$0.90 across 13 listings from 11 providers.

So the defensible claim is not "two feeds disagree". It is : **any single-source price table reports one sample as if it were the market, and the spread it hides is wider than any modelling choice in this paper.** Which provider you sign with moves the answer more than fleet sizing, precision or duty cycle. Most calculators in this space, including mine until recently, quote a point estimate.

*A related non-finding, reported because I looked for it and it is not there :* I tested whether models with few providers carry systematically worse prices. Median input price by provider depth ran $0.300 (1 provider), $0.500 (2–3), $0.190 (4–8), $0.230 (9+) — non-monotonic, confounded by capability tier. **No relationship supported.** It would have been a tidy result and it is not in the data.

## 8. What this implies, briefly

Self-hosting loses on **loaded** cost for every model in section 6, by 1.7× to 8.2×. It loses least where the model fits one card and most where it needs eleven. On **bare compute** it usually wins, which is the whole point.

The two blocks dominating the gap are not engineering skill. They are **idle capacity** — you provision for peak, a provider pools demand across thousands of uncorrelated customers — and **staffing**, where one inference engineer costs more per year than the hardware underneath them.

So the intermediate position is straightforward. Rent while you learn the shape of your demand, measure your real duty cycle, and revisit with data instead of a spreadsheet. The door does not close — weights you can download today you can still download in eighteen months, on cheaper hardware, with a faster serving stack [4].

One correction to the *non*-cost argument : "our data cannot leave" is now largely satisfiable contractually. Zero Data Retention is widely available at no per-token premium [8][11]. There is a ladder — standard API, ZDR, in-region provider, dedicated endpoint, own hardware — and most discussions jump from the first rung to the last, skipping three cheaper options. The last rung is a **physical** guarantee where the others are **contractual**. Sometimes a regulator demands exactly that. Often nobody has written down which requirement actually binds.

## 9. Limitations

Stated before reproduction on purpose. Several came from reviewers, not from me.

**The throughput data is second-hand and thinly held out.** Ten of eleven anchors from one published run [2]. n = 5 genuinely held out. Renting an H100 for two hours costs about $5 and would move this layer from `published` to `measured`.

**The parallelism finding is an assumption, not a measurement.** See 5.1.

**TPT omits KV cache and is wrong at its own anchor.** See 5.3.

**Throughput swings ~100× with batch size** and the model has no batch term. Every figure assumes a throughput-optimised configuration at high concurrency — a demand-density assumption, not a hardware property. At low concurrency the self-hosting case is *worse* than reported.

**Serving precision is observed for 16 of 55 models and inferred for 37.** Precision compounds — up to 4× on weight bytes, up to 3× on throughput — so this is a load-bearing input with uneven provenance. Corrections to that table are the cheapest useful contribution anyone can make here.

**The price median is over feed keys, not providers.** 27 of 37 comparable models carry more keys than providers (265 keys across 164 providers), so a provider publishing several aliases is over-weighted. Disclosed rather than silently fixed, because fixing it changes the published series.

**The basket's sampling frame is not fixed.** See section 4. May be fatal to the *magnitude* of finding one.

**Prices are published list prices.** Negotiated enterprise rates, committed-use discounts and free tiers are excluded, and all three favour the buyer — i.e. against self-hosting.

**GPU rental is spot-market supply.** Community capacity with no SLA [7], cheaper than anything a regulated buyer could contract. Every self-host figure is a lower bound.

**Some neocloud pricing is probably below cost.** Nothing in public data separates a subsidised price from an efficient one.

**The model prices one axis.** No fine-tuning, no multi-LoRA serving — dozens of adapters on one resident base model, which does not exist on a per-token API at any price and is a common real reason teams self-host. No latency SLO, p95 or TTFT. No prefix caching or offline batch scheduling on the self-host side, while the API side gets prompt caching and batch discounts. That ledger is one-sided and it favours renting.

**The capability tier is editorial** — the one `estimate`-grade layer, and not a benchmark score.

**The GPU set is NVIDIA-only.** No MI300X, Gaudi or TPU.

**KV-cache architecture is not modelled at all.** GQA, MLA and hybrid designs are exactly the work that attacks the block section 5.3 describes, and this tool gives them no credit.

## Appendix B — derived quantities and the assumptions inside them

Everything below is **computed by us**, not fetched. Where a constant was chosen rather than measured, it says so.

| # | Quantity | Formula / method | Assumptions — flagged |
|---|---|---|---|
| B-1 | Duty cycle from traffic shape | `duty = 1 / peak-to-average ratio` | **Exact identity, not an assumption.** But the peak-to-average ratio itself is a preset the user picks (1 / 2.5 / 4) |
| B-2 | VRAM footprint | `params × bytes-per-param × 1.3` | **The 1.3 is an assumed constant**, not measured. No context, batch, concurrency or attention term. Wrong at the tool's own anchor config (needs ~1.61) — see 5.3 |
| B-3 | KV cache | *Not modelled.* Folded into the 1.3 above | **Known gap.** Cannot represent GQA / MLA / hybrid attention. Scales with weight bytes, so quantising perversely shrinks the credited budget |
| B-4 | Fleet size | `gpusPerReplica × replicas`, replicas from peak demand | Assumes **TP adds no throughput** (section 5) — a modelling assumption, not a measurement |
| B-5 | Throughput | Bandwidth-scaled from a reference anchor; no GPU-count term | Fitted on 4 confounded anchors; bandwidth exponent from **one** GPU pair; precision speedups from **one** dense model. Held out n=5 |
| B-6 | Bare-compute floor | GPU line only, 100% utilisation, no people/overhead/idle | **A floor, not a cost.** v1's central error was quoting it as a cost |
| B-7 | Laptop vs server throughput (§2.1) | Memory bandwidth ÷ model bytes for a single stream | **Derived estimate, not a benchmark I ran.** Single-stream decode is bandwidth-bound; the comparison crosses two models and two batch sizes |
| B-8 | Break-even duty | `selfHostMonthly / (apiMonthly / duty)` | Fleet size held fixed. Reported as "never" above 100% rather than extrapolated |
| B-9 | Break-even tokens/day | `selfHostMonthly / apiPer1M`, capped at fleet capacity | Nulled beyond what the fleet can physically serve — the uncapped version was published in v1 and was wrong |
| B-10 | Price index | Mean of price relatives over a fixed basket | **Estimator choice changes the answer** — Carli 0.976, Jevons 0.840, median 1.000 (section 4) |
